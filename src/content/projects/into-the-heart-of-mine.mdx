## The idea

*First step in, last chance out.* Into the Heart of Mine is a VR journey through an ancient mine. You are a treasure hunter sent to retrieve a legendary crystal, and to complete it you have to collect shards scattered through the tunnels. Every shard brings you closer to the fortune — and deeper into a curse.

We wanted an experience where **the environment itself is the interface**. There is no HUD to read. Spatial audio, reactive lighting and shifting textures guide the player and threaten them at the same time, and the player's body becomes the controller.

## My role

I worked across the whole project, from the first idea to the final presentation:

- **Building the VR experience** in Unreal Engine — setting up the level so it ran and played in a headset.
- **Designing the environments** — the mine tunnels, shafts and caverns, and how light and colour lead the player through them.
- **3D assets** — helping create and set up the crystal shards and the pieces of the full crystal the player collects.
- **Idea and aesthetics** — shaping the concept, the mood and the visual direction with the team.
- **Presenting** — pitching the concept and showing the final experience.

## Characters

Two characters carry the story. **Elias**, the ghost of a miner, is your guide and your conscience — his voice follows you through the dark, sometimes a whisper, sometimes a scream. **The protagonist** is you: a curious soul who has to choose, shard by shard, between fortune and danger.

## Look and feel

*Dark, mysterious, and just a little cursed.* The moodboard set the palette before anything was built: amber lantern light against cold rock, and violet crystals that glow a little too brightly. Those two colours became a language — warm means safe-ish, violet means the curse is close.

<Figure src={moodboard} alt="Moodboard: mine tunnels and rail tracks, glowing purple and clear crystals, a ghostly miner with a lantern, rock textures and a palette of browns, orange, cream and teal." caption="Moodboard and colour palette." size="full" />

## Designing the player's journey

VR is physical, so the journey had to account for bodies as well as feelings. We mapped seven stages — from putting on the headset to living with the curse — and for each one wrote down what the player is trying to do, what they need from us, how they feel and what could get in their way.

That surfaced the real design problems early: confusion with controls during onboarding, getting lost or missing shards in the descent, audio that could become overwhelming, and motion sickness in the mine-cart escape.

<Figure src={journey} alt="User journey table with seven stages — Onboarding and Entry, The Descent, Collecting Fragments, Curse Escalation, Heart of the Mine, Mine Cart Escape, Curse's Toll — each with objectives, needs, feelings and barriers." caption="Seven-stage player journey: objectives, needs, feelings and barriers." framed />

Each barrier became a design response:

- **Onboarding:** a letter and an axe give the player something to read and hold before anything scary happens, so they learn the controls with their hands.
- **Getting lost:** shards glow and hum, and Elias's voice pulls the player in the right direction.
- **Unclear curse mechanics:** every shard visibly changes the mine — walls distort, lights dim, shadows move — so cause and effect are impossible to miss.
- **Motion sickness:** the escape is short and on rails, with clear obstacle cues.

## Sound: the guide in the dark

In a dark mine, the player hears more than they see, so audio carried much of the design. The team built the soundscape in Premiere Pro across 12 layered tracks — cave ambience, dripping water and rock, crystal hums, footsteps, breathing and Elias's voice — and balanced them so the player always has a cue to follow, even when the mood gets tense.

Elias's lines were processed to sound distant and unreal: a *make distant* preset, speech enhancement and de-essing, then levelled against the ambience so he cuts through without breaking the atmosphere.

## Building and playtesting

I built the mine environments in Unreal Engine: tunnels framed in timber, rail tracks that pull the eye forward, and crystal shards that act as both light source and objective. We playtested in the lab, watching where people hesitated, where they got lost and what made them jump, and adjusted the spaces in response.

## What I learned

**Design for the body, not the screen.** In VR the player is *inside* the design. Scale, ceiling height and how close a wall feels matter more than any layout on a screen. Spaces that looked fine in the editor felt cramped or endless in a headset, so I learned to check every change in VR, not on a monitor.

**Light is the interface.** With no HUD, the environment has to tell people where to go. Placing a glowing shard at the end of a tunnel, or warming the light near a safe path, guided players better than any sign. Lighting became my main wayfinding tool.

**Comfort comes first.** Motion sickness can end an experience in seconds. Keeping movement steady, giving players something fixed to look at and keeping the mine-cart ride short made the difference between tense and unbearable.

**Real-time 3D has a budget.** Detailed rocks, glowing crystals and dynamic light all cost performance, and VR needs a high, steady frame rate. I learned to balance how good an asset looks against how smoothly it runs, and to set assets up properly — scale, pivots, collision — so they behave in the world.

**Prototype early, playtest often.** Our first playtests showed problems we never saw on paper: people missed shards, turned the wrong way and froze at the wrong moments. Testing early let us fix the spaces instead of defending them.

**One vision, many hands.** A shared moodboard and user journey kept six people building the same world. Presenting the idea clearly — to the team and to the class — was as much a design skill as building it.
