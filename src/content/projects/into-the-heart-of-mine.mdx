## The idea

Into the Heart of Mine is a VR journey through an ancient mine. You are a treasure hunter sent to retrieve a legendary crystal, and to complete it you have to collect shards scattered through the tunnels. Every shard brings you closer to the fortune, and deeper into a curse.

We wanted **the environment itself to be the interface**. There is no HUD to read: spatial audio, reactive light and shifting textures guide the player and threaten them at the same time, and the player's body becomes the controller.

## Look and feel

*Dark, mysterious, and just a little cursed.* The moodboard set the palette before anything was built: amber lantern light against cold rock, and violet crystals that glow a little too brightly. Those two colours became a language: warm means safe-ish, violet means the curse is close.

<Figure src={moodboard} alt="Moodboard: mine tunnels and rail tracks, glowing purple and clear crystals, a ghostly miner with a lantern, rock textures and a palette of browns, orange, cream and teal." caption="Moodboard and colour palette." />

## Designing the player's journey

VR is physical, so the journey had to account for bodies as well as feelings. We mapped seven stages, from putting on the headset to living with the curse. For each one we wrote down what the player is trying to do, what they need, how they feel and what could get in their way.

<Figure src={journey} alt="User journey table with seven stages (Onboarding and Entry, The Descent, Collecting Fragments, Curse Escalation, Heart of the Mine, Mine Cart Escape, Curse's Toll), each with objectives, needs, feelings and barriers." caption="Seven-stage player journey: objectives, needs, feelings and barriers." framed />

## Sound: the guide in the dark

In a dark mine, the player hears more than they see, so audio carried much of the design. The team built the soundscape in Premiere Pro across 12 layered tracks (cave ambience, dripping water, crystal hums, footsteps, breathing and Elias's voice), balanced so the player always has a cue to follow, even when the mood gets tense.

<Figure src={timeline} alt="Adobe Premiere Pro project 'VR_Cave_SFX' showing a timeline with twelve layered audio tracks and a library of cave, river, crystal and breathing sound effects." caption="The VR_Cave_SFX project: 12 layered tracks of ambience, effects and voice." framed />

## Building and playtesting

I built the mine environments in Unreal Engine: tunnels framed in timber, rail tracks that pull the eye forward, and crystal shards that act as both light source and objective. We playtested in the lab, watched where people hesitated, got lost or jumped, and adjusted the spaces in response.

## What I learned

<Cards
  items={[
    { kicker: '01', title: 'Design for the body, not the screen', body: 'In VR the player is inside the design. Scale and ceiling height matter more than any layout, so I checked every change in a headset, not on a monitor.' },
    { kicker: '02', title: 'Light is the interface', body: 'With no HUD, the environment has to show the way. A glowing shard at the end of a tunnel guided players better than any sign.' },
    { kicker: '03', title: 'Comfort comes first', body: 'Motion sickness ends an experience in seconds. Steady movement, fixed reference points and a short cart ride kept it tense, not unbearable.' },
    { kicker: '04', title: 'Real-time 3D has a budget', body: 'Detailed rock, glowing crystals and dynamic light all cost frames. I learned to balance looks against performance and set assets up properly, with the right scale, pivots and collision.' },
    { kicker: '05', title: 'Prototype early, playtest often', body: 'Playtests showed what paper never did: missed shards, wrong turns, frozen players. Testing early let us fix the spaces instead of defending them.' },
    { kicker: '06', title: 'One vision, many hands', body: 'A shared moodboard and journey map kept six people building the same world, and presenting the idea clearly was as much a design skill as building it.' },
  ]}
/>
