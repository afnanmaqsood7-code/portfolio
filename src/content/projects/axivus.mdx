## Overview

Axivus turns car repair into a paperless, transparent process. It connects customers, technicians, service advisors, independent mechanics and shop owners, so a repair can go from vehicle check in to payment without a single paper form.

I was the only designer. I enhanced the existing mechanic app and designed two new ones, the advisor app and the shop owner web app, along with the flows and the shared component system behind all three. The team had two months to ship V1, so every screen had to earn its place.

</Split>

## One platform, three apps

Each person in a repair has a different job and a different device, so Axivus meets them where they work, with the same data, statuses and design language across all three.

## How a repair works

The whole product follows one simple rule: **technicians find, advisors decide, customers approve.**

1. A customer or advisor creates a repair order
2. A technician inspects the car and submits findings
3. The advisor turns the findings into an estimate with parts and labour
4. The customer approves it in the app, or from a link without the app
5. The work is done, marked ready and paid for

## Key design decisions

### 1. A command centre, not a menu

The home screen leads with the job in progress and the actions that can't wait (approvals waiting, payments to collect, customer replies), then today's route and nearby jobs. Mechanics open the app between tasks, so the next action has to be one tap away.

### 2. Bids built on trust signals

Every marketplace card shows what a mechanic needs to decide fast: the payout range, distance, and badges for a **verified customer**, **OBD codes attached** and **photos attached**. Filters for *Today* and *High payout* match how mechanics actually choose work.

### 3. Transparent estimates, with an assistant

The estimate builder shows parts, labour, margin and the mechanic's **take-home** in real time, with quick templates for common jobs. **Axi Touch** then reviews the estimate before it's sent. It groups line items, rewrites technical wording in plain language and flags sensible add-ons, then shows a clear before and after so the mechanic stays in control.

### 4. Approval without an account

Customers can approve an estimate from a secure link, with **no sign-up and no app**. Removing that step takes the friction out of the moment that most often stalls a repair.

### 5. Escrow on both sides

Customers fund the job up front into escrow; mechanics see it marked *funded* before they start. Money is released in two stages (when the repair begins and when the customer signs off), so both sides are protected, and every release appears in the receipt.

### 6. Trust you can see

Profiles show certifications, specialities, ratings and approval rate, and an **audit trail** keeps a full job history. Messages are grouped by what needs action (objections, approvals, estimates) rather than by time alone.

## One system across three apps

Designing three apps at once surfaced a key rule: there are **two kinds of job**. On a *shop job*, the advisor owns the estimate and talks to the customer, while the technician only inspects and repairs. On an *independent job*, the mechanic does everything. Making this rule explicit resolved most apparent conflicts between the apps: who sends the estimate, who the customer messages, and how they pay (in-app escrow or a payment link).

## Designing for the shop floor

Mechanics use the app with busy hands, often standing up and in a hurry. So the interface uses large tap targets, a fixed bottom bar with **Jobs** raised in the centre, consistent status colours (in progress, next up, awaiting approval) and short, plain labels. A deep navy brand with bright blue actions keeps important buttons easy to find at a glance.
