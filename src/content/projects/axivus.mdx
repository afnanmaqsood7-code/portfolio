## Overview

Axivus makes car repair paperless and easy to trust. One platform connects the customer, the technician, the service advisor at the front desk and the shop owner, so a repair can go from check in to payment without a paper form or a chasing phone call.

I was the only designer on the product. I reworked the existing mechanic app and designed two new apps from scratch: an iPad app for service advisors and a web command centre for shop owners. I also built the shared component system behind all three. We had two months to ship V1.

## The problem

Repair shops and independent mechanics run on calls, texts, notebooks and paper repair orders. Three things kept going wrong:

- **Work gets lost.** Leads, quotes, approvals and payments sit in different places, so jobs stall and nobody knows why.
- **Customers don't trust the quote.** They fear surprise charges, and approving a quote usually means another phone call.
- **Money is risky on both sides.** Mechanics buy parts up front and chase payment later. Customers pay before they know the work is done.

The goal was one clear flow for every repair, with the customer able to see and approve what is happening at each step.

## My role

As the sole product designer I owned the work from the founders' briefs to developer handoff:

- Mapped the full repair lifecycle and the roles involved
- Defined the information architecture for three apps on three devices
- Designed 20+ end to end flows, including the messy edge cases
- Designed every screen, from wireframes to high fidelity
- Built one component library and status language shared by all apps
- Worked with the founders on scope and with developers on handoff

## How I worked

1. **Understand the job.** I worked from the founders' briefs and broke a repair down into each step, who does it and what they need to see.
2. **Map the roles.** I wrote down what each role does, owns and must never see, which became the rules for every app.
3. **Flows first.** I designed each flow end to end before polishing screens, including what happens when things go wrong.
4. **One system.** I built shared components and states early, so three apps could be designed at the same time without drifting apart.
5. **Review and ship.** Regular reviews with the founders, then annotated files for developers.

## The rule that shaped everything

Every repair follows one line: **technicians find, advisors decide, customers approve.**

1. A customer or advisor opens a repair order
2. A technician inspects the car and submits findings
3. The advisor turns the findings into an estimate with parts and labour
4. The customer approves in the app, or from a link without an account
5. The work is done, marked ready and paid for

Designing three apps at once also showed that there are **two kinds of job**. On a *shop job*, the advisor owns the estimate and talks to the customer, and the technician only inspects and repairs. On an *independent job*, the mechanic does it all. Writing this rule down settled most of the arguments between apps: who sends the estimate, who the customer messages, and how they pay.

## Mechanic app (iPhone)

Mechanics use the app standing up, with busy hands, between jobs. Everything is built around one journey, with a five step tracker on every active job: **Hired, Drop off, Inspection, Estimate, Repair.**

**Home shows what can't wait.** The job in progress comes first, then approvals waiting, payments to collect and customer replies, then today's route. The next action is always one tap away.

**Approval without an account.** Customers approve from a secure link with no sign up and no app. This removed the step where repairs most often stalled.

**Escrow protects both sides.** The customer funds the job up front and the mechanic sees it marked *funded* before starting. Money is released in two stages, when the repair starts and when the customer signs off.

**Trust you can see.** Profiles show certifications, specialities, ratings and approval rate. Messages are grouped by what needs action, and every job keeps a full history.

## Service advisor app (iPad)

The advisor sits between the customer and the technician: answering calls, opening repair orders, building estimates, chasing approvals and taking payment. The app runs on a shop tablet at the counter, with a sidebar as the only navigation.

<Figure src={ipadInbox} alt="Advisor inbox on iPad: approvals waiting with what the customer said next to what the technician found, a delay risk flag, today's schedule and messages." caption="The inbox leads with decisions, not information." framed />

- **Action first.** The inbox opens on approvals waiting, no response and payments due. Every screen has one clear next step.
- **Both sides of the story.** Each repair order shows what the customer said next to what the technician found.
- **Delays are flagged.** Risks like a part on backorder appear as a clear flag with the suggested next action.
- **Nine clear states.** Needs estimate, Sent, Viewed, Approved, In progress, Waiting parts, Ready, Completed and Paid, worded the same everywhere.

## Shop owner command centre (web)

The command centre is where an owner starts every shift. The brief was strict: answer "what do I do next?" in under three seconds.

<Figure src={webCommand} alt="Shop owner command centre: four cards for needs approval, needs parts, waiting payment and in progress, an urgent strip of aging repair orders, blockers and a tech snapshot." caption="Money at risk, what is blocked and who is busy, in one view." framed />

- **Numbers that lead somewhere.** Each card shows a count and a dollar value and opens the matching list in one click.
- **Age you can see.** Approvals and payments are sorted by age, and timers turn orange then red, so the oldest money at risk stays on top.
- **Capacity at a glance.** A calendar shows bays and technicians at capacity, next to a board of repair orders by stage.
- **Beyond the happy path.** Every list has empty, loading and error states, with an inline retry instead of an error page.

## One design system

Three apps on three devices had to feel like one product.

- **One status language.** The same job and payment states, colours and chips on phone, tablet and desktop.
- **Built for the shop floor.** Large tap targets, a fixed bottom bar with Jobs raised in the centre, short labels and screens readable at arm's length.
- **A clear brand.** Deep navy with bright blue actions, so the important button is always easy to find.
- **Axi Touch everywhere.** The estimate assistant appears wherever an estimate is sent, and always sorts work into fix now, fix soon and later.

## Designing the edge cases

Real repairs rarely go to plan, so I designed for what goes wrong as carefully as the main flow:

- **Scope changes mid job.** The mechanic finds more work, sends a new estimate and the customer approves the change.
- **Top up payments.** When the cost goes up, escrow is topped up before work continues.
- **Objections.** A customer can question a line item, and the conversation stays attached to the job.
- **Customers without the app.** Approvals and payments work from a link.

## Reflection

**What I learned**

- **Design the edges early.** Scope changes and objections are where real jobs fail, and designing them first made the product believable.
- **Trust is a feature.** Escrow, guest approval and a visible history did more for the experience than any single screen.
- **Write the rules down.** The "shop job or independent job" rule saved more time than any component.

**What comes next**

- **Test on the shop floor** to see how the apps hold up with gloves, noise and interruptions.
- **Measure the funnel,** from bid to hire and from estimate to approval, to find where jobs stall.
