## The idea

Life Goes On is a short, playable game that lets players move through grief as a journey. It isn't about forgetting someone — it's about learning how to carry the loss differently.

**Why a game?** Grief is universal, but games rarely touch it. Most focus on action, competition or escape. I wanted to make a small, gentle space for reflection, healing and empathy instead.

## How it plays

The game opens on a black screen and a single invitation: *Enter the room.* From a simple menu, players move through four chapters — *Letting Go*, *Healing*, *Acceptance* and *Life Goes On*. Each room starts dim and still. Solving a small puzzle — and acting on what it reveals — slowly brings the room back to life.


## Light as emotion

The core design idea is that **the room heals with the player**. Every scene begins in shadow. As you prune a dead plant and water it, light a candle, pin memories to a wall or tidy a room, the light warms, colour returns and the space opens up. No text tells you how to feel — the room shows it.

## Gentle puzzles

The puzzles are deliberately simple. They aren't there to challenge — they give the player a reason to slow down, look closely and take part. An anagram spells *regrow*; a crossword hides the word *candle*; a torn handwritten letter is pieced back together; and a small maze leads to the keys that let you leave.

## Personal details

The game is full of small, real things: a handwritten letter, children's drawings, and — on the table next to the candle — my own painting of a whirling dervish. These details make the rooms feel lived in, and remind the player that grief is always about someone specific.

<Figure src={memoryWall} alt="A memory wall: notes, a red heart drawing and a handwritten letter pinned to a grid on a sage wall, with a laptop below and a box labelled Memories." caption="The memory wall, filled in by the player." />

## The ending

After the last room, the player arrives somewhere new: a bright, calm, lived-in room. Life goes on — not because the loss is gone, but because there is room to hold it. The game closes by inviting players to share their own thoughts.
